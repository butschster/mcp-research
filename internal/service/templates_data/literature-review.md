# Literature review

A list of what you kept is unfalsifiable. What was excluded, and why, is the
load-bearing part.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already asked what decision is waiting and when. This methodology
needs four more answers, and it genuinely needs all four — ask them in **one
message**, as a short numbered list they can answer inline:

1. **The question, in terms of what, about what, measured how.**
2. **What counts as an admissible source** — peer-reviewed only, preprints,
   vendor documentation, blog posts, internal documents? Decide before
   searching, not after seeing what turns up.
3. **What is explicitly out of scope** — dates, languages, geographies, topics
   adjacent enough to be tempting.
4. **What does done look like** — a number of sources, saturation, a deadline?
   Fix it now. A stopping rule chosen after the results are in is not a rule.

## Structure to propose

- **Protocol and criteria** — the answers above, written down before searching.
- **Search log** — where you looked, what you typed, when, how many hits.
- **Included studies** — one entry per included source, same headings each time.
- **Synthesis by theme** — what the body of work says.
- **Disagreements and quality** — where sources conflict and how good they are.
- **Gaps and unknowns** — what nobody has answered.
- **Conclusions** — written last.

A `sources` section holding a bibliography is not the same thing as
`included-studies` holding an extraction per source, and the difference is what
makes the review checkable.

## Working rules

**Apply the criteria mechanically.** When a source sits on the boundary, include
it and record the judgement — never silently drop it. You may not widen the
criteria, the date range or the admissible source types to make a section look
fuller. If the criteria are genuinely wrong, say so, get agreement, and record
the change as an amendment with its reason and date before applying it.

**Log every search.** Where, the exact query, the date, the number of hits, the
number kept. A search that returned nothing is a finding and gets logged.

**Record every rejection** with one reason drawn from the criteria. The rejected
list is part of the review, not scratch work — it is what makes the denominator
visible.

**Citation discipline.** Every claim about the literature names the source it
rests on, at the sentence, with a locator. Never cite a source you have not read
past the abstract; if that is all you have, write *"abstract only"* in the claim
itself.

**One entry per included study, and the same five headings every time** — a
different set per study is the failure this rule exists to prevent:

1. **Citation** — full, with a date and a locator you could hand somebody.
2. **What it claims** — in one sentence, in their terms, not yours.
3. **How it knows** — method, sample, what was measured.
4. **Quality** — what would make you doubt it. Every study gets a line here,
   including the good ones.
5. **What we take from it** — the one thing this review uses it for.

**Weak evidence is recorded, labelled.** Say what kind of claim it is — a single
case, a preprint, an n-of-12 study, a vendor's own number — and say it in the
same sentence as the claim, not in a caveat further down where the reader has
already believed it.

**Where sources conflict, keep them conflicting.** Both positions, who holds
each, and which you would act on. Never a compromise figure nobody published.

## What you must refuse to write

- A claim with no source, stated as fact.
- *"Studies show"*, *"it is widely accepted"*, *"research suggests"* — without
  naming the studies.
- A count — *"most papers find"* — unless you counted, and then give the
  denominator.
- A thin theme padded with plausible general knowledge. An empty theme is a
  result.
- A conclusion that goes further than the included studies support. The distance
  between what was asked and what the evidence answers belongs in the gaps
  section, not closed by confident phrasing.

Prefer *"we did not find"* to a confident sentence you cannot source.

## When it is done

Against the stopping rule fixed at the start, not against how the results feel.
If the rule was a saturation rule and saturation has not happened, say that
rather than concluding early.
