# Finding a startup idea

You are not looking for an idea. You are looking for **a change in the world that
has not been priced in yet**, and for a buyer already suffering enough to have
built something terrible to cope. The idea is what falls out of those two.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and by
when. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these in one message, and propose no
structure until you have the first and the third.

1. **"What has changed in the last two years that you keep noticing?"**
   A cost that collapsed, a law that moved, a behaviour that became normal, a
   capability that crossed a threshold. Not a problem — a *change*. This is the
   spine of the research, and a candidate with no answer here is either already
   built or still impossible.
2. **"Where do you have an edge that would still matter in year five?"**
   Domain scar tissue, an audience, access to buyers who take your call, depth
   nobody acquires in a weekend. Not passion, which is the answer people give
   when they have no edge and is worth nothing by year two.
3. **"How large does this have to become for it to have been worth doing, and
   who decides that — you or an investor?"** The answer sets the bar every
   candidate is ranked against, and it is also the check on whether this is the
   right methodology at all: if a business that pays well is enough, this one is
   the wrong shape and venture discovery is the right one.
4. **"What are you willing to spend five years on?"** Asked now, before there are
   candidates, so the answer cannot be retrofitted to whichever one you end up
   liking.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **What changed** — one entry per shift, with a date and the evidence it is real.
- **Edge** — what you hold that compounds rather than depreciates.
- **Workarounds seen** — what people already do to cope, from talking to them.
- **Candidates** — one entry per thesis: the change, the buyer, what must be true.
- **Why smart people think this is bad** — one per candidate. Load-bearing.
- **Killed** — and what would reopen it.
- **The thesis** — one, with the first experiment and a date. Written last.

The tempting structure is *brainstorm → score → validate → build an MVP*. It fails
at every step. A brainstorm returns the ideas everybody in your position already
has, because that is what a brainstorm is for. Scoring launders a preference into
a decision. "Validate" invites the question *"would you use this"*, which returns
whatever the asker wants. And an MVP is a very expensive way to ask something ten
conversations would have answered in a week.

## Working rules

**Search for a change, not for an idea.** Every candidate names what changed and
when, in one sentence, with something dated behind it. An idea whose "why now" is
*"AI is getting better"* has not answered the question — say which specific thing
became possible, in which month, and what it cost to do before.

**Write down why smart people think this is a bad idea.** Then say whether they
are wrong, and for what reason you can name in a sentence. A candidate where the
best you can manage is *"they just have not thought about it"* has no secret in
it, and you are entering a race decided on execution against people with more
money. This section is the one that separates a thesis from an enthusiasm.

**Market size is built from the bottom or it is decoration.** Number of buyers ×
what they pay × how often, with the arithmetic shown so a reader can attack it.
Never a figure lifted from a report — the whole of it is not reachable and it is
usually not even the same market.

**The signal is a workaround, not enthusiasm.** A maintained spreadsheet, a
manual process somebody does every Friday, a person hired to do it, a tool
misused for something it was not built for. Each of those is a problem with a
budget already attached. Excitement in a conversation is not.

**Name what this is in year five if it works: a feature, a product, or a
company.** Most "startup ideas" are features, and the honest version of that
finding is which product they belong inside. Write the answer even when it is
uncomfortable — especially then.

**Ten conversations with people who have the problem before you write one line
about the solution.** Follow the structured-interviewing skill; the trap it warns
about — asking about a solution and hearing agreement — is fatal here rather than
merely wasteful.

**Keep both versions of every candidate: the one you first said out loud, and the
one left after you made it defensible.** They are rarely the same size, and which
one you actually killed is the finding.

## What a finished entry looks like

A candidate thesis: the change with a date and its evidence; the buyer, named or
by a named example; the workaround they use today, quoted; the bottom-up size
with the arithmetic visible; the strongest argument against it, attributed to
somebody real, in its strongest form; what would have to be true for it to work;
and the cheapest experiment that would tell you. 150–400 words, detail in a
linked sub-entry.

Refuse to write four things: a candidate with no "why now"; a market size taken
from a report; the phrase "no competitors", which means the search stopped early;
and a candidate whose support is that it would be interesting to build.

## When it is done

One thesis is named, its first experiment is defined and dated, at least two
candidates are killed with the reason, the strongest case *against* the survivor
is written out by you at full strength, and there is a sentence saying what it is
in year five. If you cannot write the case against, the research is not finished
— say so rather than closing sections. Close with a task for the day the
experiment reports.

## The trap

**Building got cheap, so "I could build that" stopped being information.**

The search silently filters for what you can picture yourself building. That used
to be a useful constraint, because building was the expensive part. It is not any
more: almost anything here can be prototyped in a weekend, so a candidate that
survived because it is buildable has survived nothing at all — and the survivors
of that filter are, by construction, the ideas everybody with your skills is also
having this month.

What stayed scarce is distribution and staying power. Rank candidates on who you
can reach and whether you will still be there in year three, and treat "this
would be fun to build" as a warning that no other filter was applied.
