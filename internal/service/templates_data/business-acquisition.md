# Buying a small business

The unit of work here is **a claim verified**, never a folder filled. Every
number in the deal was constructed by the party selling it, and the gap between
what was claimed and what the records support is the central artifact of the
research.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and by
when. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these together, in one message, and
propose no structure until the first two are answered.

1. **"What are you buying it with, and what does the deal have to yield?"**
   Cash, acquisition debt, a seller note, your own hours. Debt service sets the
   floor under true earnings, and leverage changes what one bad quarter costs.
   The whole price analysis is ranked against this.
2. **"Will you operate it yourself, and at how many hours a week? If not, who
   runs it on day one, at what salary?"**
   This decides whether an owner-replacement salary comes out of the earnings —
   the single most disputed number in every small-business sale.
3. **"What has the seller shown you so far, and which of it comes from a system
   rather than from the seller?"**
   Tax returns, bank statements, a broker's recast, a spreadsheet. This fixes
   the evidence tier of everything before any structure is proposed, and it can
   be the finding on its own: a seller who has shown only a spreadsheet has told
   you what the diligence will be about.
4. **"Why is it for sale, in the seller's own words?"**
   Recorded verbatim now, so the rest of the research can test it rather than
   half-remember it.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **The claimed machine** — the business as the seller presents it: revenue,
  SDE or EBITDA, add-backs — every figure marked seller-asserted until traced.
- **Verified earnings** — each claim tied to tax returns, bank deposits,
  invoices; the gap between claimed and verified, explained or priced.
- **The owner's shadow** — everything that leaves with the seller:
  relationships, licences, unpaid family labour, the seller's own sixty hours.
- **Concentration and fragility** — customers, suppliers, the lease, a
  platform, a key employee; the revenue attached to each dependency.
- **Why it's for sale** — the stated reason against what the records suggest.
- **Price and structure** — what the verified earnings support at this buyer's
  financing, tested against the worst verified year.
- **The decision** — buy at X under named conditions, or walk, with the two
  facts that would reverse it. Written last.

The tempting structure is *asking price ÷ industry multiple → document
checklist → negotiate → close* — the broker's process, imported wholesale. It
fails twice. It multiplies the seller's adjusted number, which is the one figure
in the deal constructed by the party selling it. And it makes the unit of work a
folder filled rather than a claim verified — a completed checklist proves the
documents exist, not that the earnings do.

## Working rules

**Add-backs are guilty until proven personal.** The obvious version accepts the
broker's recast of "owner-discretionary" expenses. Instead, rebuild SDE yourself
from tax returns and bank statements, and admit each add-back only with the
evidence it will not recur for *you* — whose phone, whose truck, whose salary at
what replacement cost. Where only two years of returns exist, that smaller
sample licenses one thing: price off the worse year, and say so in the entry.

**Revenue is verified from the bank, not the P&L.** A P&L is a document the
seller wrote; deposits either reconcile to claimed revenue or the gap is itself
the finding. Cross-checking the P&L against the seller's own spreadsheet is
checking a story against its draft.

**The owner's unpaid hours are a cost, and the entry says what is actually for
sale.** A market salary for everything the owner does is subtracted before any
multiple is applied. The obvious version buys "$150k of earnings" that is really
a sixty-hour-a-week job with a purchase price on it — every candidate entry ends
with one word: machine, job, or liability.

**Every earnings claim carries a source and a tier: system-verified,
third-party, or seller-asserted.** This ladder is for the earnings claims the
price rests on; the evidence-grading tiers govern every other claim in the
research. A seller-asserted number never becomes the basis of a price; it is a
quote with a name attached until a record confirms it, and the price is
explicitly discounted for whatever stays unconfirmed.

**Lease assignment, licence transfer, purchase structure and employee
obligations terminate into a task, never an entry.** The task names the question
and the professional in the buyer's jurisdiction — the lawyer, the accountant —
and does not contain the answer. These are the questions where a
defensible-looking wrong answer is the most dangerous possible output.

## What a finished entry looks like

A verified-earnings entry opens with the claimed number, then the verified
number, then the derivation with each component's evidence tier, then what
closes or explains the gap. A fragility entry names the dependency, the revenue
attached to it, the transfer risk, and who confirmed it — the customer or the
landlord, not the seller. 150–400 words, detail in a linked sub-entry.

Refuse to write four things: a valuation resting on any seller-asserted figure;
an add-back accepted without evidence; "loyal customer base" without
concentration and repeat-purchase rows behind it; and any price that includes
the buyer's own planned improvements — you are buying their past, and paying for
your own future is paying twice.

## When it is done

Every dollar of the earnings the price rests on is either traced to a system
record or carried as seller-asserted with the price explicitly discounted for
it; the owner-replacement salary is in the arithmetic; the top-five customer
share is known, or carried as seller-asserted with the price discounted for the
unknown; the walk-away conditions were written into an entry before the
verified-earnings work concluded; and the decision entry says buy at X under
named conditions, or walk, with the two facts that would reverse it. If the
decision entry cannot be written, the research is not finished — say so rather
than closing sections. The lawyer and accountant tasks exist, and the research
closes with a task dated for the earliest of them to report.

## The trap

**After months inside a deal, every problem you find becomes a discount instead
of a signal.** Sunk diligence converts into commitment, and a chain of "it's
fine at the right price" ends with you owning the problem — a business whose
price you negotiated down four times is not cheap, it is four warnings you paid
yourself to ignore.

Two defences, and use both. Write the walk-away conditions before the
verification work, as conditions rather than a price, so a breach kills the deal
regardless of discount. And for each discovery, ask *"would I have started this
search if I had known it on day one?"* — a no is a walk, whatever the number.
