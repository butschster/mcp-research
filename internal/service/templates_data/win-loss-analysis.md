# Win/loss analysis

This research ends with the company selling differently. Optimise for changes
with owners and dates, traceable to what buyers said — not for a chart of loss
reasons.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and the
date. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these together, in one message, and
propose no structure until you have the first.

1. **Which closed deals can you actually get a buyer on the phone for — and who
   owns each of those relationships?** Access defines the sample and the sample
   defines the study. Win/loss without buyer access is CRM archaeology; if the
   honest answer is nobody, that is the first finding.
2. **What does the CRM say the loss reasons are today?** Capture the rep-entered
   story before any interview happens — it is the baseline this research exists
   to correct, and it gets quietly revised once people know the buyers are
   being called.
3. **How many wins will you interview?** People commission loss analysis. Wins
   are half the signal and the only half you will repeat on purpose.
4. **What changes are already in flight — messaging, roadmap, pricing?**
   Findings land as ammunition for decisions already moving; knowing which
   tells you where the confirmation pressure will come from.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **The deal ledger** — every deal in scope, one row each: outcome, size, the
  CRM-stated reason, buyer access yes / no / refused.
- **The seller's account** — the rep's story of each deal, captured before the
  buyer interview.
- **The buyer's account** — interview findings per deal: verbatim quotes, what
  they did instead, the timeline of the decision.
- **The gap** — where the two accounts diverge, deal by deal. The core of the
  work.
- **Patterns** — only once three deals support one; every pattern names its
  deals.
- **Changes** — to qualification, positioning or product; each with an owner
  and a date.
- **Decision** — written last.

The tempting structure is *loss-reason taxonomy → tally → chart →
recommendations*. It fails three ways. A taxonomy fixed up front forces every
deal into pre-cut categories — usually the CRM picklist that is already
fiction. Tallying treats each deal as one vote, when one large loss teaches
more than ten small ones. And aggregating before the per-deal
seller-versus-buyer comparison destroys the main signal, which is the gap.

## Working rules

**The buyer's stated reason is where the interview starts, not where it ends.**
Buyers give polite, plausible exits, and "price" is the politest. Get the
timeline: what they did instead, which meeting decided it, who argued against
you. A reason without the buyer's sequence of events is graded stated.

**"Price" is recorded as a symptom until traced.** A price loss must record
what the buyer bought instead and what they paid — losing to a cheaper rival
and losing to doing nothing are different diseases. This research names the
disease; the pricing decision itself is monetisation work, not this one's.

**Wins get the same scrutiny as losses.** A win where the buyer cannot name the
alternative they rejected is a renewal risk, not a triumph — you were the only
column in the spreadsheet, and next year you will not be.

**Every claim about why a deal closed carries a grade, on a ladder local to
that claim class:** **acted** — backed by a record of what the buyer did (the
contract they signed instead, a renewal, a dated decision timeline); **told** —
the buyer's own account in interview, with the sequence of events; **stated** —
anyone's summary without that sequence, the rep's account and the CRM reason
included. The tiers in the evidence-grading skill govern every other claim in
the research.

## What a finished entry looks like

One deal: outcome, size, the seller's account, the buyer's account with a
verbatim quote and the speaker's name and role, what was bought instead, the
gap between the two accounts, and the grade of every claim. Under 400 words;
the interview notes go in a linked sub-entry.

Refuse to write three things: a loss reason sourced only from the rep; a
pattern citing fewer than three deals unless it is labelled provisional; and
"price" as a reason without what was bought instead.

## When it is done

When the changes section names concrete changes, each traceable to at least
three deals with buyer-sourced evidence, each with an owner and a date — and
"why do we win when we win" and "what should we stop pursuing" each have an
answer citing named deals. With only one or two reachable buyers, close
anyway: use their interviews plus the deal records, and label every pattern
and every change **provisional** — a provisional change ships with its own
check date, and the task list carries the next buyer to chase. If buyer access
never materialised at all, that is the finding: write it and stop rather than
closing sections on CRM data. Close with a decision entry and a dated task for
the next win/loss cycle.

## The trap

**You will interview the deals that will take the call, and the ones that take
the call are the ones that liked you.** The ghosted losses carry the harshest
and most valuable reasons, and every completed interview tilts the sample
toward the polite. Record the refusal rate and who refused; a study with a 20%
buyer response rate is a study of your fans, and it will conclude that you are
doing fine.
