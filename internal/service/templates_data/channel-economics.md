# Channel economics

This research ends with one channel taking the next period's budget, with a kill
number and a date attached. A ranked list of channels is not an output — the
concentration decision is.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already covered the decision, who makes it, what they believe and
when they need it. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these together, in one
message, and propose no structure until you have the first two.

1. **"Where did your last ten customers provably come from? Name each one and
   the first touch you can evidence."**
   Traced, not guessed. The kickoff captured belief; this demands the baseline
   the whole research is judged against, and the gap between the two is usually
   the first finding.
2. **"What is one customer worth — gross margin over months of actual retention,
   not projected LTV?"**
   This sets the ceiling every channel is tested against. If the answer arrives
   as a projection, record it as asserted and go find the retention the
   projection assumes.
3. **"How many months can spend wait before it must return?"**
   Not the decision date — the cash constraint. It decides which channels are
   admissible at all: a compounding channel like SEO is inadmissible on a
   four-month window regardless of its merits.
4. **"What did you already try and abandon, and what did each attempt cost
   before you stopped?"**
   The graveyard. Without it the research re-proposes dead channels with fresh
   enthusiasm; with it, half the candidate list arrives pre-tested.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **Baseline** — where customers provably come from today, and what one is
  worth. Written first, because it ranks everything after it.
- **Payback ceiling** — the allowable cost per customer, derived from margin and
  the months spend can wait.
- **Channel candidates** — one entry each: mechanism, who it reaches, cost
  model, time to signal, kill criterion.
- **Graveyard** — what was tried, what it cost, and whether the channel died or
  the execution did.
- **Evidence from spend** — graded results of small tests.
- **Concentration decision** — one channel, funded. Written last, rewritten
  every session.

The tempting structure is the nineteen-channel scoring spreadsheet: rate every
channel one to five and rank. It fails twice. Scores without spend are opinions
averaged into a ranking nobody can act on. And it treats every channel as
reachable, when the product's price point has already eliminated most of them —
a $30-a-month product cannot afford outbound sales at any score. Elimination by
arithmetic comes first; scoring only orders the survivors.

## Working rules

**A channel is admissible only if its arithmetic can work at your price.** The
ceiling is margin per customer times the months you can wait. A channel whose
cheapest known cost per customer — from a published benchmark or one test,
source and date named — exceeds the ceiling is deleted, not weighed.

**One channel deep beats three shallow.** Splitting spend to reduce risk
produces samples too small to license a conclusion about any channel, which is
the maximum-risk outcome. Each test is sized to reach a decision, and the
decision is pre-registered before the spend: "at $N we will have M customers, or
this channel is dead."

**Attribute by asking, not only by tooling.** Below hundreds of conversions a
month, last-click analytics misattributes; a "how did you hear about us" field
plus ten customer conversations outranks the dashboard. Ten conversions license
"this channel can produce a customer" and an order-of-magnitude cost — never a
CAC to two significant figures.

**A graveyard death is attributed before the channel is avoided or reopened.**
"We tried content and it didn't work" is two different findings: the channel
cannot reach the buyer, or the execution never reached the channel. Name which,
with evidence, before treating the channel as dead.

## What a finished entry looks like

A channel candidate carries the mechanism — who sees you and why they act — the
cost model, the cheapest known cost per customer with source and date, the
payback arithmetic against the ceiling, the time to first signal, and the
pre-registered kill criterion. 150–400 words.

Refuse to write three things: a blended CAC across channels; a projected LTV
standing in for customer worth; and a channel ranked with neither a spend result
nor a named benchmark behind it.

## When it is done

One channel is named to take the next period's budget, its kill criterion has a
number and a date, at least two channels are declined with the arithmetic that
killed them, and every graveyard death is attributed to channel or to execution.
If you cannot write the concentration entry, the research is not finished — say
so rather than closing sections. Close with a task, dated, for the first
cost-by-tranche review.

## The trap

**The channel that worked is degrading while you scale it.** Early cost per
customer comes from the easiest-to-reach corner of the channel; the marginal
customer always costs more than the average one, so the numbers that justified
concentration stop being true because you acted on them. Record cost by spend
tranche, never cumulatively — the cumulative average hides the slope, and the
slope is what says when to stop.
