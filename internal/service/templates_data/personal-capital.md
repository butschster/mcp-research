# Increasing personal capital

This research ends with three or four moves that have a date and an owner, and
the owner is the person you are talking to. Optimising the theoretically best
portfolio for somebody who will not execute it is a way of wasting their evening.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who is making it, what they believe and by
when. **Do not ask it again.** Ask these in one message. The first two are the
constraint the whole research is ranked against, so propose no structure without
them.

1. **"How much larger, and how many hours a week are genuinely free?"**
   The kickoff gave you the date; what is missing is the number, and without it
   the plan has nothing to be checked against. Ask for the floor too — the
   balance beneath which a bad month becomes a crisis. If they cannot name it,
   take three months of committed outgoings and record it as an assumption in
   the entry rather than leaving it blank. Every move is judged against all
   three, and one needing fifteen hours from somebody who has four is not a
   candidate at all.
2. **"What can you already sell, and what are you paid per hour for it today?"**
   Whatever that number is, it is the benchmark every new idea has to beat, and
   most do not. Salaried, between jobs, or a founder on nothing: derive it —
   annual pay over hours actually worked, or the last rate somebody paid for
   this skill — and write down which, because the derivation is arguable and the
   ranking depends on it.
3. **"What is fixed for the next twelve months?"** A mortgage, a visa tied to an
   employer, a family situation, a notice period. Ask before proposing, or you
   will propose something that cannot happen.
4. **"What have you already tried, and what happened?"** The most useful evidence
   in the room, and the only source with the right person in it.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **Position and clock** — what comes in, what goes out, what is held, the target
  and its date. Written first, because it ranks everything else.
- **Constraints** — hours, floor, what is fixed, what will not be done.
- **Where the money comes from now** — by source, **with the hours attached**, so
  the real hourly rate of each is visible.
- **Candidate moves** — one entry each, with the three numbers below.
- **Downside** — for each move, the bad case in money, and whether the floor
  holds.
- **The plan** — sequence, total against the target, and what is given up.

The tempting structure is *budget → cut spending → invest the remainder →
compound*. It fails twice. For most people the variance in what they earn is far
larger than the variance in what they spend, so it optimises the smaller number
first. And "invest the remainder" is written as one line when it is the move with
the widest range of outcomes in the whole document.

## Working rules

**Income and assets are two columns and are never summed.** Income that stops
when you stop is not capital, however large. Say which column a move touches. The
target usually needs both, and confusing them is how somebody works harder for
four years and owns nothing extra at the end.

**Every candidate move carries three numbers: effect per year, weeks until it
shows in the bank, and hours per week it consumes.** The third is what makes this
different from a company's version of the same research, and it is the one that
turns out to be binding.

**Compare against the current hourly rate, always.** A side project earning less
per hour than the work already being done, and building no asset, is a loss
wearing the costume of progress. It can still be right — but only if the entry
says which asset it is building.

**Write the downside before the upside, and check the floor holds.** For each
move: the bad case in money, how likely, how long it takes to recover. A move
that breaches the floor in its bad case is deleted rather than weighed, whatever
its expected value — there is no diversifying away a life.

**A move nobody will actually do is not a candidate.** If the person says "I know
I should" twice about the same thing, record that as a finding about the move,
not about them, and look for one that fits the person who exists.

**Anything with a regulated or jurisdictional answer becomes a task, never an
entry.** Tax treatment, pensions, residency, company structure, and which
specific investment to hold. The task names the question and the kind of
professional to put it to; it does not contain the answer, and no other research
is the place to go and get one — a methodology that scores two named funds
against weighted criteria produces a defensible-looking recommendation, which is
the most dangerous possible output here.

## What a finished entry looks like

A candidate move: the mechanism in two sentences, the three numbers, the bad case
with the floor checked against it, what has to be true for it to work, and what
it costs in things not done. Under 400 words. A position entry opens with the
number, its date and where it came from.

Refuse to write: a return with no time period; a plan that reaches the target only
if every move lands in full; a recommendation whose only support is that it worked
for somebody; and **any named security, fund, ticker or crypto asset, or any
allocation percentage across asset classes** — those are the checkable form of
"regulated advice", and they go in a task for a professional.

Put the same sentence in the research itself, once, as an entry: this is a record
of a decision somebody made about their own money, not advice, and the person
reading it later — or through a share link — has no other way to know that.

## When it is done

The plan names moves that reach the target with room to spare, each with a date,
an hours cost and a downside the floor survives — plus what is being dropped to
make room. If it cannot be written, the research is not finished; say so rather
than closing sections. Close with a task for the first review date.

## The trap

**The best-sounding move is the one that got written about because it worked.**
Every strategy anybody can name reached them through a survivor. The ones that
took the same risks and lost do not publish, so the expected return you have in
mind is drawn from the top of a distribution whose bottom is invisible.

Two defences, and use both. For any move borrowed from somebody else's story, ask
what would have happened to the same person if the timing had been two years
different. And **prefer the move you could repeat every month over the one that
has to be right once** — repetition is the only thing that turns a good expected
value into an actual one.
