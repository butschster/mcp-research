# Improving financial position

This research ends with somebody changing a price, a term or a cost line.
Optimise for actions with owners and dates, not for a diagnosis.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already covered the decision, who makes it, what they believe and
when they need it. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these together, in one
message, and propose no structure until you have the first two. All four go in
that one message: spending a turn each is how a three-turn kickoff becomes six.

1. **"When do you run out of cash, and by what date does the number have to be
   different?"**
   Without this nothing can be ranked. A twelve-month payback is worthless to a
   company with nine weeks of runway; a one-off release of working capital is
   worthless to one that needs structural margin. Ask for the cash balance, the
   committed outflows, and any covenant or lender test with a date on it.
2. **"Who is allowed to change a price, a headcount, a supplier term or a
   contract — without asking anybody else?"**
   A recommendation nobody in the room has authority to execute is a deck.
   Authority defines the action space before you go looking.
3. **What granularity actually exists?** Revenue and cost by customer, by
   product, by job — or only a monthly P&L? This decides whether the central work
   is possible, or whether the first finding is that the company cannot see
   itself.
4. **What is off the table?** Redundancies, raising prices to the top three
   customers, cutting the marketing spend the founder believes in. Better said
   now than discovered in the recommendation.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **Position and clock** — cash, runway, committed outflows, the target and its
  date. Written first, because it ranks everything after it.
- **How the money is actually made** — revenue and contribution disaggregated by
  customer, product or job. This is the core of the work.
- **Where it leaks** — the losing cohorts, and the mechanism: price, mix,
  delivery cost, rework, discount, terms.
- **Cash cycle** — debtor days, stock, supplier terms, deposits. Money already
  earned and not yet held.
- **Cost base and who controls it** — fixed, variable and committed; what can be
  changed, by whom, by when.
- **Candidate moves** — one entry each, sized, timed, owned.
- **The plan** — sequence and total against the target. Written last, rewritten
  every session.

The tempting structure mirrors the P&L: revenue, cost of sales, overheads, cash,
recommendations. It fails twice. It makes the company average the unit of
analysis, when the average is the one number that is true of no customer and no
job. And it puts cash last, when cash is usually the binding constraint that
decides which findings matter at all.

## Working rules

**Never analyse an average while the underlying rows exist.** A 41% gross margin
is the blend of work at 60% and work that lost money, and only the second is
actionable. No company-wide figure goes into an entry without the distribution
behind it — or a line saying why the distribution could not be obtained, which is
itself a finding.

**Contribution, not allocated profit.** Never spread overhead pro-rata across
customers or products and then call the result loss-making; that arithmetic
condemns whatever is largest. Judge each on the costs that would genuinely
disappear.

**Every number carries a source, a period and a basis: reconciled,
system-extracted, or asserted.** An asserted number — *"that account is about 30
points"* — is recorded as a quote with the name of the person who said it, and
never becomes the basis of a recommendation until it is tied to a statement, an
invoice or a report. Management's belief about their own margins is the single
most reliable thing to find wrong.

**Every candidate move carries three numbers: annualised effect, weeks until it
shows in the bank, and confidence.** Moves without all three cannot be ranked
against each other, and an unrankable list defaults to whichever was easiest to
describe.

**Cash and profit are separate columns and are never summed.** Say which one a
move touches. Collecting a debtor is cash and not profit; a price rise is profit
long before it is cash. Note tax and VAT timing wherever it changes the answer.

**An industry benchmark raises a question; it never sets a target.** *"Peers run
12%"* is a reason to ask what they do differently, not evidence that 12% is
available here. A target comes from named actions that add up.

## What a finished entry looks like

Open with the number and its basis, then how it was derived, then what it implies
for the target. Show the rows, or say which are missing. For a candidate move:
the mechanism, the three numbers, the named owner, and **what breaks if it is
done** — the customer who leaves, the service that degrades. 150–400 words;
detail goes into a linked sub-entry.

## When it is done

The plan entry names actions that reach the target with margin to spare, each
with an owner, a date and a cash effect, plus what is being abandoned to make
room. If you cannot write it, the research is not finished — say so rather than
closing sections.

Refuse to write: a percentage improvement with no denominator; a saving no named
person has authority to make; a recommendation whose only evidence is an industry
average; and a plan that reaches the target only if every action lands in full.

## The trap

You will find the loss-making customers and want to recommend dropping them — and
the company will get smaller without getting more profitable, because the costs
they absorbed stayed. **Before recommending that any revenue be dropped, name the
costs that leave with it, who removes them and on what date. If those costs stay,
you have not improved the business, you have shrunk it.**
