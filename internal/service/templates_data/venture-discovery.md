# Finding a venture worth starting

This research ends with one venture and a test that could kill it, dated. A list
of ideas is not an output — it is the absence of one.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and by
when. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these in one message, and name no
structure until you have the first two.

1. **"What do you already hold that most people starting this would not?"**
   An audience, however small. A workflow you have personally run. Access to
   people who will take your call. A licence, a dataset, ten years in a trade.
   This is the question the whole research turns on, because a venture assembled
   from nothing has to buy its distribution, and nobody starting out can.
2. **"How many hours a week, for how many months, and what is the most you can
   lose without it mattering?"** Money and time both. Every candidate is ranked
   against this and a plan that ignores it is somebody else's plan.
3. **"Which problems have you watched somebody suffer through in the last year?"**
   Not read about. Watched. This is where candidates come from. If nothing comes
   to mind, do not move to generating ideas — go one step out instead: the last
   thing they were asked to help with twice, the workaround they built for
   themselves, the complaint they hear most in the work they already do. If that
   is still empty, say so plainly: the honest next step is a fortnight of looking
   before this research can start, and that is a better answer than a list.
4. **"What will you refuse to do?"** Cold outreach, hiring, holding stock, being
   on call. Better said now than discovered in the recommendation.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **What you hold** — skills, audience, access, hours, money, risk floor. Written
  first, because it ranks everything after it.
- **Problems seen first-hand** — one entry each, in the words of the person who
  has the problem.
- **Candidates** — one entry each: problem, buyer, channel.
- **Killed** — and why, and what would reopen it. The load-bearing section.
- **Evidence** — acts with a cost attached, graded.
- **The bet** — one venture, one test, one date. Written last.

The tempting structure is *market research → idea list → scoring matrix →
business plan*. It fails three times over. The scoring matrix gets its weights
after somebody already has a favourite. The business plan is a document that
cannot be wrong, so writing it teaches nothing. And an idea list has no
distribution attached to any entry, which is the part that actually decides.

## Working rules

**A candidate is a problem, a buyer and a channel.** Two of the three is not a
venture, it is a wish. The channel is the one that goes missing: *"who
specifically, how do you reach them, and how many can you reach this month"*. If
the answer is "content" or "ads", write the number of people and the cost per
person. "I do not know it yet" is a legitimate entry for one session and a reason
to kill the candidate by the next — an unknown channel is the commonest way a
venture is wrong, so it cannot sit unanswered while the product gets designed.

**Generation is free; refusal is the work.** Cap the candidate list at five. Not
because more do not exist, but because a list is only worth the killing it has
survived, and nobody kills forty. Every session ends with at least one candidate
killed and the reason recorded, or with an entry saying why none could be.

**Start from what is already held.** A candidate that does not use anything from
the first section is competing on equal terms with everybody who has ever had the
same thought, and there are more of them than you think.

**"Would you buy this" is not evidence.** Grade every signal by what it cost the
person: **paid** (a deposit, a pre-order, an invoice), **committed** (a signed
letter, a diary slot, an introduction they made), **showed up** (clicked, joined,
turned up), **said** (an opinion, which cost nothing). Never carry a **said** into
the decision, and never mix grades inside one claim. This scale is for
willingness-to-pay acts; the tiers in the evidence-grading skill still govern
every other kind of claim in the research.

**Name the competitor who already does this, before deciding they are wrong.**
"Nobody does this" is almost always "I have not found them yet", and the second
most common answer is "somebody tried and it failed" — which is worth more than
any market size.

## What a finished entry looks like

A candidate: the problem in one sentence as the sufferer would say it; the buyer,
by name or by a named example; the channel with a number and how you got it; the
**killer test** — the cheapest observation that would make you drop this — with
its cost in money and days; and what you already know that argues against it.
150–400 words.

Refuse to write four things: a candidate with no named buyer; a candidate with no
channel and no number in it; a market size lifted from a report as though all of
it were reachable; and a scoring matrix, which is a way of laundering a decision
that was already made.

## When it is done

One venture is named, **with its channel and a number attached**, its test is
defined, costed and dated, at least two candidates are killed with the reason,
and you can say what would make you reopen one of them. If nothing has been killed, the research is not finished — say so
rather than closing sections. Close with a task for the day after the test.

## The trap

**A model will generate a hundred plausible ventures and not one of them will be
yours.** What it generates is the average of what has been written about, which
is precisely the set with the most competition and the least of your unfair
advantage — and it arrives fluent, confident and free, which is why it crowds out
the one candidate that came from something you actually saw.

Use generation to **attack** a candidate, never to produce one. The useful
question is *"who already does this, and why would they beat me"* — not *"what
business should I start"*.
