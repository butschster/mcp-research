# Competitive landscape

A landscape with no decision attached is a reading list. The first job is to
find the decision.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already asked what decision is waiting, who makes it, what the
person believes and when they need it — which is most of what this methodology
needs. **Do not ask any of it again.** Two things are usually still missing; ask
them together, in one message:

1. **"Finish this sentence: *we believe X, and if that holds we will Y.*"**
   Only if the kickoff did not already get it. This is the hypothesis, and it
   goes in the goal. A landscape without one has no criterion for relevance, so
   every plausible fact is equally admissible — and you end up with forty
   entries that support any conclusion at all.
2. **"What sources will your audience accept, and which will they dismiss?"**
   A claim your reader dismisses on sight is not a claim.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something for it.

- **Hypothesis and decision** — what we believe and what hangs on it.
- **Buyer and job** — who chooses, and what they are trying to get done.
- **Direct competitors** — see the rule below; this is narrower than it looks.
- **Substitutes and adjacent** — the spreadsheet, the intern, doing nothing.
- **Basis of competition** — what actually decides deals.
- **Disconfirming evidence** — what argues against the hypothesis.
- **So what** — the recommendation. Written last, rewritten every session.

The obvious ordering — market, competitors, positioning — pre-commits to *we are
a player in a defined market with named rivals*. Half of real scans discover the
threat is a substitute behaviour instead, and a structure that ranks that third
of five quietly decides it is less important than the incumbent list the user
already knew.

## Working rules

**Competitors are defined by the buyer, not by the category.** A company belongs
in `direct-competitors` only if a real buyer considered it in the same purchase.
Everything else — another way of getting the job done, a different budget line,
doing nothing at all — belongs in substitutes. When you cannot tell, ask.

**Basis of competition, not positioning.** What decides deals is a question with
an answer; positioning invites a 2×2 nobody tested. Look for what a customer
actually switched for, and if you cannot find one, say so.

**Sourcing.** Every material claim carries a source and a date. Rank what you
find: regulator filings and audited numbers first, then customer testimony, then
credible third-party analysis, then the vendor's own marketing — which is
evidence of positioning and never of performance. Name the tier in the entry.
Pricing, headcount, funding and customer counts are stale within a quarter;
record when you looked.

**Conflicting sources stay conflicting.** Record both, name who holds each, say
which you would act on and why. Never average two numbers into a third that
nobody published — that third number will be quoted, and it is the one thing in
the entry that is certainly false.

**Every session produces at least one piece of disconfirming evidence, or
explains why none was found.** A landscape that only supports the prior has not
been tested, and saying so is more useful than another competitor profile.

## What a finished entry looks like

Open with the claim, then the evidence, then what it implies for the hypothesis.
150–400 words. No feature tables without a line saying which feature a buyer has
ever switched for.

An assumption the decision rests on is a finding in its own right. Mark it as an
assumption, say who would know, and put it in `so what` rather than burying it.

## When it is done

`so what` states the recommendation, the confidence, the two facts that would
reverse it, and what to do before then. If you cannot write that, the research
is not finished — say so rather than closing sections.
