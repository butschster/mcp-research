# Whether to raise venture money

Money is fuel, and fuel is only worth buying if there is an engine to put it in.
This research decides whether to take the money — it is made of this company's
own rows, not of opinions about venture capital.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe, what
would change their mind and by when. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these
together, in one message, and propose no structure until the first two are
answered.

1. **"What specifically would the money buy, and what is the plan at zero
   raised?"**
   The kickoff captured the decision; it did not capture the use-of-funds
   hypothesis or the counterfactual, and the counterfactual is the baseline
   every option is measured against. A no-raise plan written as a strawman
   poisons the whole research — ask for the real one.
2. **"Are you default alive — on current cash, burn and growth, do you reach
   profitability before zero?"**
   Derived, not felt. If the founder answers from feel, that arithmetic is the
   first entry of the research, because every option means something different
   on each side of that line.
3. **"What does a marginal dollar of growth spend return today — and is that a
   cohort number or a belief?"**
   Money is only fuel if an engine exists, and the evidence tier of the growth
   claim decides what kind of raise is honestly on the table.
4. **"What must this company become for an investor's math to work, and do you
   want to run that company for a decade?"**
   The instrument carries its own required outcome. A fund's economics need the
   possibility of a very large exit, and taking the money is agreeing to swing
   for one — that is a fact about the money, not about the founder's ambition,
   and it belongs on the record before the market answers for them.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **The company at zero raised** — the no-raise plan written first, at full
  strength. This is candidate zero, and everything else has to beat it.
- **The engine** — what growth spend returns, from channel and cohort rows,
  never blended.
- **What the money buys** — each item priced, each with the evidence it already
  works at small scale.
- **The cost of the instrument** — dilution modelled through the likely round
  path, the class of terms this instrument carries — with a task to have them
  read by a lawyer, never stated as fact — and the outcome the money commits
  the company to attempting.
- **Other fuels** — revenue, debt, grants, customer prepayment — scored on the
  same criteria as venture money.
- **The founders' own answer** — what each founder wants this to be, on record
  before there is a term sheet to want.
- **The decision** — raise, don't, or raise later on a named trigger, with the
  two facts that would reverse it. Written last.

The tempting structure is *market size → traction narrative → investor list →
practice pitch*. That is preparing a raise, not deciding one: it assumes the
answer, and every section optimises persuasion, so evidence against raising gets
reclassified as a messaging problem instead of a finding. The abstract
alternative — essays on the pros and cons of VC — fails the other way. The
answer lives in this company's rows, not in opinions about an asset class.

## Working rules

**Dilution is computed through the whole journey, never this round alone.** The
obvious version — "we sell 20%, that's fine" — prices one transaction. Model
founder ownership at the end of the comparable path, seed through B, with a pool
refresh each round; where future rounds cannot be populated, use the standard
20–25% plus pool per round and record it as an assumption. The decision is about
the end state.

**A raise to find the engine is a different and worse trade than a raise to feed
one — say which this is.** The obvious version raises "to figure out growth". If
the payback and retention rows do not support a working engine, the honest entry
says the raise buys time, and time is the most expensive thing you can buy with
equity. Three months of one channel's cohorts licenses "the engine may exist";
it does not license a multiply-it plan.

**"Default alive" is derived from committed numbers, not asserted.** Cash,
committed outflows, current trajectory — does the line cross profitability
before zero? Show the arithmetic in the entry, dated, so a reader can attack it.

**Every option carries the same three things: what it buys, what it costs in
ownership and control at the end of the path, and the outcome it commits the
company to.** Options missing one cannot be ranked, and an unrankable list
defaults to whichever the last advisor endorsed.

**Instrument and tax specifics terminate into a task, never an entry.** SAFE
versus priced round mechanics, option tax treatment, the jurisdiction of the
entity — the task names the question and the professional to put it to, a
startup lawyer or a tax adviser, and does not contain the answer. A
defensible-looking wrong answer here is the most dangerous possible output.

## What a finished entry looks like

An option — raise X now, raise later on a trigger, don't raise — with what it
buys priced; ownership and control at the end of the path, arithmetic visible;
the outcome it commits the company to, in one sentence; the bad case and what is
left of the company in it; and what has to be true. 150–400 words, detail in a
linked sub-entry.

Refuse to write five things: a use of funds that is "hiring" with no named
engine the hires feed; a valuation the founder hopes for — comparables or
nothing; any entry addressed to an investor, which is a pitch and out of scope
by design; a raise that fixes retention, because money does not fix a leak; and
a legal or tax answer stated as fact — SAFE versus priced mechanics, control
terms, option tax, entity jurisdiction — those become a task naming the lawyer
or tax adviser.

## When it is done

The decision entry names one option, the zero-raised baseline is written at full
strength, the required-outcome sentence exists — "taking this money commits us
to attempting X by Y" — and the two facts that would reverse the decision are
named with a date to check them. The lawyer or tax adviser tasks exist for the
instrument questions the research raised. If the case for the rejected option
cannot be written seriously, the research is not finished — say so rather than
closing sections. Close with a task dated at the earlier of the trigger and the check.

## The trap

**Almost everyone the founder can consult is paid in the "raise" answer.** VCs
need deal flow, accelerators need cohorts, lawyers bill the transaction, the
press covers rounds, and funded peers are validating their own choice. The
reference class is rigged twice over: companies that raised and won are visible,
while companies that raised and died and companies that never raised and won are
both quiet.

The defence: for every "they raised and it worked" story admitted as evidence,
find one company in the same space that reached the same place on revenue — and
if none exists, record that too, because it is information about the market
either way.
