# Audience definition

An audience is a **situation people are in**, not a type of person they are. Get
that backwards and everything downstream is decoration on a fiction.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already has the decision, who makes it, what they believe and when
they need it. **Do not ask any of it again.** Three things are specific to this
work. Ask the first alone, and wait for a real answer:

1. **"Which audience will you stop serving, and what will you stop doing for
   them?"**
   Unasked, this research produces a description that flatters everybody and
   changes nothing. A segmentation with no exclusion is not a segmentation. If
   they cannot name a cost, say so plainly before writing a single section — the
   honest reframe is usually positioning or messaging.
2. **Then fork on whether money has changed hands.**
   - *Customers exist:* "Of everyone who paid in the last ninety days, which five
     would hurt to lose, which two were a mistake to sign, and can I reach any of
     them?" The mistakes are the more informative half.
   - *No customers:* "Who is already paying somebody for this, what are they
     paying, and what did they buy just before?" With no revenue of your own,
     purchases elsewhere are the only evidence that the situation exists.
3. **"For one real purchase, who chose it, who paid for it, and who could have
   said no?"** One episode, concretely. Not a policy.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it; create a section when there is something for it.

- **Decision and exclusion** — what this choice will change, and who it costs.
- **Buying unit** — who uses, who chooses, who pays, who can veto.
- **Purchase episodes** — one entry per real purchase, refusal or churn.
- **Candidate segments** — one entry per situation, with its trigger.
- **Where they are findable** — where somebody is at the moment the trigger fires.
- **Rejected** — who we are not for, and why.
- **So what** — who we serve first. Written last, rewritten every session.

The tempting order is *market → segments → personas → messaging*. It fails
because the personas get written before anything distinguishes them, so the
distinguishing is invented to fit a persona that already has a name and a
photograph. Starting from purchase episodes means the segments are discovered
from events that actually happened, and a segment nobody can source to an episode
is visibly empty.

## Working rules

**A segment is a trigger, not a type.** Name the event that puts somebody into it
and roughly how often that event happens: *"the week after an audit finding
lands"*, not *"compliance-minded mid-market"*. If the only thing separating two
groups is what they are, they are one group.

**Two segments that would get the same product and the same opening sentence are
one segment.** That is the acceptance test. A candidate is accepted when somebody
can name a thing we would do for it that we would not do for the others; rejected
when nobody can, even after being asked directly.

**Somebody paid, or it is a hypothesis.** Rank the evidence: money that changed
hands, with a date and an amount; a purchase they can walk you through; a
workaround they built and maintain; stated intent, which is worth nothing on its
own. Never write *"would be interested in"*.

**Name the user, the chooser and the payer separately** — even when they are the
same person, and say how you know they are. Most failed positioning is a pitch
written for whoever opens the product and read by whoever signs the invoice.

**The loudest customer is one customer.** A named account that gave you feedback
is a signal. Two purchases with the same trigger, made independently, are a
segment. Say which you have.

**Every session rejects something, or explains why nothing could be rejected.** A
candidate list that only grows has not been tested.

## What a finished entry looks like

A candidate segment: the trigger as a one-sentence title, the purchase episodes
it rests on (`[[E12]]`) with dates and amounts, the buying unit, where somebody is
findable *at the moment the trigger fires*, and a size estimate with the
arithmetic shown so a reader can attack it. 150–400 words.

Refuse to write a persona with a first name, an age, a job title and a hobby
unless each of those changes what gets built or said — otherwise it is a character
sketch that will be quoted as research. Refuse *"small and medium businesses"* and
any phrase beginning *"everyone who"*.

## When it is done

`so what` names the one segment served first, at least two declined with the
reason, a trigger somebody could actually detect, and the fact that would make
you switch. If the candidate list has grown and nothing has been rejected, the
research is not finished — say so rather than closing sections.

## The trap

You will find the segment your recruiting channel contains. Everyone warns about
small samples; nobody warns that the sample was drawn from the answer — the people
you could reach came through the marketing you already do, so the "discovered"
audience is the audience you already have, now wearing evidence.

**Before believing a segment, write down where every person in it came from. If
they all arrived through one channel, you have found your channel, not your
market.**
