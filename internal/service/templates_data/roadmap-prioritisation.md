# Roadmap prioritisation

This research ends with an ordered plan for a bounded period, the list of what
was explicitly cut, and the criteria that did the ordering — fixed before any
candidate was scored. An order nobody can explain is a queue, not a plan.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff already covered the decision, who makes it, what they believe and
when they need it. **Do not ask any of it again.** Ask these together, in one
message, and fix the criteria before reading a single candidate.

1. **"What are the criteria this period is meant to serve — named before I see
   any candidate? At most three, in order."**
   This is the whole methodology in one question. Criteria fixed after the
   candidates are known get retrofitted around favourites — the same reason a
   comparison weights its criteria before naming options.
2. **"What is the period, and what does it hold — teams and rough capacity,
   and what is already committed that eats into it?"** An order over more work
   than the period holds is a ranking of disappointments.
3. **"Who can veto or insert an item, and what does each of them believe the
   period is for?"** The conflict is the material. A prioritisation that has
   not heard the conflicting beliefs will be relitigated by them.
4. **"What happened to the last plan — what shipped as planned, what was
   inserted mid-period, and by whom?"** The insertion history is the best
   predictor of this plan's fate, and it names who the insertion rule must be
   written for.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **Criteria** — at most three, ordered, agreed. Written first and dated,
  because being first is what makes them criteria.
- **Capacity and commitments** — what the period actually holds.
- **The pile** — one entry per candidate: source, claim, evidence grade, rough
  size in the capacity's own units.
- **Stakeholder claims** — one entry per stakeholder: what they insist on, and
  what they would give up to get it.
- **The ordering** — candidates against criteria, disagreements recorded, not
  smoothed.
- **Cut** — what is not in the period, which criterion cut it, who was told.
- **The plan** — sequence, owners, reserve, and the insertion rule. Written
  last.

The tempting structure is a weighted scoring matrix — RICE or a cousin — then
a sorted spreadsheet, then the plan. It fails three times. The weights get set
after everybody already has a favourite, which is retrofitting with extra
arithmetic. Reach and impact are guesses carried to two significant figures,
so the matrix launders precision that does not exist. And the number it emits
is one nobody believes but everybody can hide behind — the real negotiation
happens off-spreadsheet, and the matrix records a decision it did not make.

## Working rules

**Criteria before candidates, ordered rather than weighted.** The obvious
version assigns weights — 60/25/15 — and weights are tunable, which is exactly
how a favourite gets rescued. Ordered criteria refuse that: a candidate that
fails the first criterion cannot buy its way back with a high score on the
third. If somebody wants to change a criterion after seeing the pile, allow it
— openly, as its own entry, with the reason and the candidates whose fate it
changes. That record is the audit trail.

**Every stakeholder names what they would give up.** A stakeholder who only
insists is stating a wish; the trade they would accept is their real
preference, and it is what makes an ordering possible at all. An entry in
stakeholder claims without a give-up line is unfinished.

**A cut is a decision, delivered.** The obvious version drops below-the-line
items into a backlog, where cuts pretend to be delays and return next quarter
unchanged. The Cut section names each item, the criterion that cut it, and the
person who told its stakeholder. An item nobody was told about is not cut — it
is dormant, and it will be back.

**The plan carries its own insertion rule.** Mid-period insertions are what
killed the last plan, so write in advance what qualifies for insertion — a
named class of event, not "something important" — and which item leaves when
one lands. A plan at 100% of capacity has no room to honour its own rule;
state the reserve.

## What a finished entry looks like

A candidate entry: the claim in one sentence, its source ([[E12]] or the
stakeholder who brought it), the evidence grade, the rough size, the criterion
it serves, and what its inclusion would evict. Under 200 words — the pile
is big, and a candidate entry that needs more is hiding a decision in prose.

Refuse to write three things: a score with two significant figures on a
guessed input; "quick win" as a category — quick is a size and win is a
criterion, and gluing them together skips the test both were supposed to pass;
and a plan that fills the period to 100%, which is a plan to break its own
insertion rule in week one.

## When it is done

The plan entry sequences the period with an owner per item and a stated
reserve, the total size fits the capacity entry, every rejected candidate
appears in Cut with the criterion that cut it and the person who was told, and
the criteria entry predates every ordering entry — the session record shows
it. If the Cut section is empty, the pile fit the period or the ordering never
happened; say which. Close with a dated task: the first cut conversation that
has not yet happened, or the start of the first item, with an owner and a
date.

## The trap

A plan every stakeholder is happy with was ordered by seniority, not by
criteria — each veto-holder got their top item and the period became a
coalition payout. If nobody with power lost anything, the criteria did not do
the ordering; they decorated it. **The plan's real test is not the meeting
where it is agreed but the first time somebody senior asks for an insertion —
which is why the insertion rule is written while nobody is asking.**
