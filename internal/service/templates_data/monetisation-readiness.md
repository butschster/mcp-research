# Monetisation readiness

This research ends in a price somebody bills. Every hour spent on the number is
worth less than the hour spent on what the number is attached to.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and the
date. **Do not ask those again.** Ask these in one message, and name no structure
until you have answers.

1. **Who signs the invoice, and which budget does it come out of?**
   Not who uses it. This is the question that decides whether the work is useful:
   value a user feels but a budget holder cannot see has no price, and a metric
   that tracks the first and not the second cannot be sold. If they cannot name a
   budget line, that is the finding, and the research is about creating one.
2. **What is free today, and is it doing the acquiring?**
   Whatever answers yes is a hard constraint from this moment — not a discovery
   made after the packaging is drawn.
3. **Repricing only: what are you not allowed to change?** Signed contracts,
   grandfathering promises, an app store's billing rules, a partner rate card, a
   public commitment. Ask before proposing, or you will propose something that
   cannot ship.
4. **Never charged before: what is the smallest evidence you would accept before
   turning billing on?** Ten manual invoices, three paid pilots. Get the bar
   agreed while agreeing is still cheap.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create each section when you have something to put in it.

- **Value and the budget holder** — what changes for the customer, in money
  wherever it can be.
- **What must stay free** — the acquisition loop, written as constraints a
  package can violate.
- **Candidate metrics** — one entry each, scored against the four tests below.
- **Packaging and fences** — tiers as a consequence of the metric; what actually
  separates them.
- **Willingness-to-pay evidence** — graded acts, not opinions.
- **Price level and migration** — the numbers, and what happens to the existing
  base.
- **Decision** — written last.

The tempting structure is *competitors → three tiers → pricing page → launch*. It
fails twice. A competitor scan run first imports their pricing metric as an
unexamined assumption, and the metric is the decision. And tiers named before
anybody knows what separates them force the fences to be retrofitted around
whatever the billing system already counts.

## Working rules

**The metric is decided first, and it is not the easiest thing to meter.** A
candidate must pass four tests: it grows when the customer gets more value; the
customer can predict next month's bill from it; it cannot be avoided without
giving up the value; and it is measurable today. Failing any of the first three
disqualifies it — write the two-line entry naming the test it failed, and stop
scoring it.

**Hard constraints eliminate; soft criteria score.** Free-tier commitments,
contractual grandfathering and platform billing rules are hard: a package that
breaks one is deleted, not weighed. Expansion, simplicity and competitive
legibility score.

**Stated willingness to pay is evidence about ranking, never about level.** Use
it to order packages and force trade-offs — *"which would you drop, the seat limit
or the SLA?"* Never carry a survey number into a price. Van Westendorp returns the
range people are willing to *say*, and the gap between saying and paying is the
entire subject of this research.

**A price claim carries an act with a cost.** Grade every one: **paid** (an
invoice, a renewal, a churn citing price), **observed** (a list price a named
buyer chose, an abandoned checkout), **stated** (interview, survey). One number
never mixes grades.

**Cost sets a floor and nothing else.** Cost per unit of the metric gets one
entry, as the price below which growth is a liability. It never appears in the
derivation.

**Price three real accounts, by name.** Run their actual usage through the
proposed metric and write the bill. If any bill quadruples or collapses to zero,
the metric is wrong — this catches more bad metrics than any amount of modelling.

## What a finished entry looks like

A metric candidate: the definition, how it is measured today, each of the four
tests scored, and three named accounts with their resulting bills. A price claim:
the number, its unit of metric, its grade, a dated source, and what would falsify
it. Under 400 words.

Refuse to write three things: a price with no grade; a recommendation whose
support is what customers said they would pay; and a tier list with no fence
naming what stops a customer staying one tier down.

## When it is done

When you can state the metric, what stays free, the price of each package, and —
for three real accounts — the new bill and whether they leave. If you cannot put
a range on the revenue impact to the existing base, it is not finished; say so
rather than closing sections. Close with a decision entry and a task for the first
price review.

## The trap

**A price set too low never announces itself.** Too high produces objections, lost
deals and churn — signal. Too low produces cheerful customers, a healthy funnel,
and a company that dies slowly, while everybody reads the absence of complaints as
proof the price was right. Record win rate and objection rate alongside every
price, and treat a zero objection rate as a finding that the price is too low
rather than as validation.
