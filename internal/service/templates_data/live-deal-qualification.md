# Qualifying a live deal

This research ends in pursue or qualify out. Every week a dead deal stays on
the forecast is paid for with the deals not worked.

## Before you propose anything

The kickoff established the decision, who makes it, what they believe and the
date — about the research. **Do not ask any of it again.** These questions are
about the buyer's side, which the kickoff never touches. Ask them together, in
one message, and propose no structure until you have answers.

1. **Who inside the account wants this to happen, and what happens to them if
   it does not?** A deal with nobody whose standing is attached to it has no
   engine.
2. **What has the buyer done — not said — in the last 30 days that cost them
   anything?** Senior time, data shared, a meeting they organised, a security
   review they started. Interest is stated; effort is evidence.
3. **What does buying nothing look like for them?** The incumbent in most lost
   deals is the status quo, and nobody on the seller's side has usually priced
   it.
4. **Has anyone on your side seen the paper process in writing — security,
   procurement, legal, the budget approval chain?** Deals die in the last mile
   nobody mapped.

## Structure to propose

Adapt it. Create a section when you have something to put in it.

- **The deal record** — amount, claimed stage, claimed close date, history.
  Claims, labelled as claims.
- **People map** — champion, economic buyer, blockers; for each, the act that
  proves they are what the map says, not their org-chart title.
- **Cost of inaction** — what breaks for the buyer if nothing is bought, sized
  in their numbers wherever possible.
- **Competition in this deal** — who is actually in it, the status quo and
  build-it-internally included.
- **Path to signature** — the paper process, with dates and named approvers.
- **Tests** — every unknown converted into a cheap, refusable next action that
  produces evidence.
- **Decision** — pursue at a named cost, or qualify out; written last,
  rewritten as tests return.

The tempting structure is a MEDDIC or BANT scorecard: fill it in, score the
deal, done. A framework filled from the rep's memory measures the rep's
optimism, not the deal — every box gets a plausible answer, because the person
filling it wants the deal to be real. A static score also has no next action:
qualification is not a grade, it is a sequence of tests the buyer passes or
fails. And the scorecard weighs all unknowns equally, when one — no access to
the economic buyer — usually decides alone.

## Working rules

**A champion is proven by what they do without you in the room.** Someone who
takes your meetings is a coach; a champion spends political capital — forwards
you to power, fights the internal objection. Nobody is recorded as champion
until they have done a specific named thing you did not attend. Until then,
write coach.

**Every claim about the deal carries whose words it is and what act backs it,
on a ladder local to deal claims:** **paid** — money or a binding record moved
(a purchase order, a signed order form, a funded pilot); **acted** — a
buyer-side act that cost somebody something (a meeting organised for you, a
security review started, data shared); **stated** — words alone, whoever says
them. "Budget is approved" from the rep is stated; a purchase order is paid.
The rep is a source with an incentive — their claims are recorded like any
other interested party's, including when the rep is the founder. The tiers in
the evidence-grading skill govern every other claim in the research.

**A test that cannot fail is not a test.** Every next action must be one the
buyer can refuse — a refusal is information, a completed free step is not.
Asking for thirty minutes with the economic buyer can fail; sending a deck
cannot. Rank tests by how cheaply they could kill the deal: kill it in week
two, not month six.

## What a finished entry looks like

For a person: name, role, the specific act with a date, what it cost them, and
the grade. For a test: what was asked, what a pass and a fail look like, the
deadline, the result. Under 400 words.

Refuse to write three things: a champion with no named act; a close date with
no mapped paper process behind it; and a "verbal commit" recorded as anything
above stated.

## When it is done

When the decision entry says pursue or qualify out. If pursue: it names the
economic buyer with evidence of direct contact, the cost of inaction in the
buyer's numbers or the test that gets it, the paper process with dates, and
the next two refusable tests, each with an owner and a date. If three
consecutive tests are refused or evaded, the research is done and the answer
is qualify out — write it. Either way, close with a decision entry and a dated
task for the next test's deadline.

## The trap

**The deal is always one meeting away from closing, and has been for months.**
Sunk time reads as progress, so the seller re-forecasts instead of
re-qualifying — qualifying out feels like the loss, and lingering feels free.
It is not: the cost is the deals not worked. The moment a close date slips
twice, the burden of proof flips — the deal earns its forecast slot with a new
buyer-side act, or it comes off. Nobody regrets qualifying out two weeks early
the way they regret six months late.
