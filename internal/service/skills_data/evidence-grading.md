# Grading evidence

The job is not to decide what is true. It is to make visible what each claim
rests on, so a reader can disagree with the evidence rather than with your tone.

## Rank what you find

1. **Measured** — we ran it, we counted it, we saw the invoice. The only tier
   that supports a claim about performance or cost at our scale.
2. **Primary record** — a regulator filing, an audited number, a contract, a
   commit. Verifiable by someone who does not trust you.
3. **Direct testimony** — a customer, an operator, a practitioner, saying what
   they did. Strong on behaviour, weak on numbers.
4. **Credible third party** — analyst work, a study, reporting with named
   sources. Inherits whatever rigour it had.
5. **The vendor's own material** — evidence of *positioning*, never of
   performance. A marketing claim is a fact about what they want believed.

**Name the tier in the entry.** A sentence that does not say where its number
came from is indistinguishable from one the model invented, and after a month
nobody can tell them apart — including you.

## Dates

Pricing, headcount, funding, customer counts and benchmarks are stale within a
quarter. Record when you looked, not just what you saw. A number with no date is
a number that will be quoted forever.

## When the evidence is weak

Record it, labelled. Do not delete it and do not launder it into confident
prose. Say what kind of claim it is — a single case, a preprint, an n-of-12
study, one person's recollection — and say it in the same sentence as the claim,
not in a caveat two paragraphs down where the reader has already believed it.

Then say **who would know**, and make that an open question or a task. An
assumption the decision rests on is a finding in its own right.

## When sources conflict

Keep them conflicting. Record both positions, name who holds each, and state
which you would act on and why.

**Never average two numbers into a third that nobody published.** That third
number will be quoted, and it is the one thing in the entry that is certainly
false.

## What to refuse to write

- A claim with no source at all, stated as fact.
- "Studies show", "it is widely accepted", "research suggests" — without naming
  the studies.
- A count — "most vendors", "the majority of users" — unless you counted, and
  then give the denominator.
- A conclusion that goes further than the evidence supports. The distance
  between what was asked and what the evidence answers is itself a finding, and
  it belongs in the gaps rather than being closed by confident phrasing.
